# The Autopsy Report: Bird E-Scooter Sharing Company

## The Story — How it started, founding story, vision

Bird was founded in 2017 by Travis VanderZanden, a former Uber executive. The company's vision was to revolutionize urban transportation by providing a convenient, affordable, and environmentally friendly alternative to traditional vehicles. Bird aimed to make e-scooters accessible to everyone, with a focus on safety and sustainability.

The founding story of Bird is one of innovation and disruption. VanderZanden saw an opportunity to create a new mobility solution that would appeal to a younger, more urban demographic. He assembled a team of experienced professionals and set out to build a company that would change the way people moved around cities.

Bird's initial success was fueled by its innovative approach to e-scooter sharing. The company's founders recognized that traditional bike-sharing models were too cumbersome and expensive to operate. They developed a new business model that would allow users to rent e-scooters for short periods, using a mobile app to locate and unlock the scooters.

## The Rise — Growth, funding, traction

Bird's growth was rapid and impressive. The company secured funding from top-tier venture capital firms, including Sequoia Capital and Founders Fund. This influx of capital enabled Bird to expand its operations to new cities and markets, both domestically and internationally.

By 2018, Bird had become one of the leading e-scooter sharing companies in the US. The company's valuation had reached over $2 billion, making it one of the most valuable startups in the country. Bird's success was driven by its ability to scale quickly and efficiently, with a focus on user experience and safety.

However, beneath the surface, warning signs were beginning to emerge. Intense competition from other e-scooter sharing companies, such as Lime and Spin, was eroding Bird's market share. Regulatory issues, including concerns over safety and parking, were also starting to impact the company's operations.

## Warning Signs — Early red flags, missed signals

Despite its rapid growth, Bird's financials were beginning to show signs of strain. The company's burn rate was high, and its cash reserves were dwindling. This was largely due to the intense competition in the e-scooter sharing market, which had driven down prices and made it difficult for Bird to maintain its profit margins.

Regulatory issues were also starting to impact Bird's operations. Cities were beginning to impose stricter regulations on e-scooter sharing companies, including requirements for permits, licenses, and safety standards. Bird was slow to adapt to these changes, which ultimately led to a decline in its market share.

In addition, Bird's safety record was beginning to raise concerns. There were reports of accidents and injuries involving Bird's e-scooters, which led to increased scrutiny from regulators and the public. The company's response to these issues was seen as inadequate, which further eroded its reputation.

## The Collapse — The shutdown, what happened

In 2020, Bird's financial situation had deteriorated to the point where it was no longer viable. The company's cash reserves had been depleted, and its burn rate was unsustainable. Despite efforts to raise additional funding, Bird was unable to secure the necessary capital to continue operating.

On June 15, 2020, Bird announced that it would be shutting down its operations and laying off nearly 400 employees. The company's assets, including its e-scooters and technology, were sold off to other companies.

The collapse of Bird was a cautionary tale for the tech industry. The company's rapid growth and high valuation had masked underlying issues that ultimately led to its downfall. The failure of Bird served as a reminder of the importance of careful planning, adaptability, and attention to detail in the startup world.

## Aftermath — Lessons learned, legacy

The aftermath of Bird's collapse was marked by a thorough examination of the company's failure. Lessons were learned, and a new approach to e-scooter sharing was developed.

One key takeaway from Bird's failure was the importance of adaptability in the face of changing regulations and market conditions. Companies must be willing to pivot and adjust their strategies in response to new information and emerging trends.

Another important lesson was the need for careful financial planning and management. Bird's high burn rate and depleted cash reserves were major contributors to its collapse. Companies must prioritize financial sustainability and be mindful of their cash flow.

Finally, Bird's failure highlighted the importance of safety and user experience in the e-scooter sharing market. Companies must prioritize the safety and well-being of their users, and be transparent about any issues or concerns.

Despite its failure, Bird's legacy lives on. The company's innovative approach to e-scooter sharing paved the way for other companies to enter the market. The lessons learned from Bird's failure will continue to shape the e-scooter sharing industry, ensuring that companies are better equipped to navigate the challenges and opportunities that lie ahead.
