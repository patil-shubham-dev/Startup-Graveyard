# The Autopsy Report: Varo Money

## The Story — How it started, founding story, vision

Varo Money was founded in 2013 by Colin Walsh, a former Bank of America executive. Walsh's vision was to create a digital bank that would provide affordable and accessible financial services to underserved communities. Initially, the company focused on providing a mobile banking app that would allow users to manage their finances on their smartphones. Varo Money aimed to disrupt the traditional banking industry by offering a more user-friendly and cost-effective alternative.

### Early Days

In its early days, Varo Money secured funding from investors such as Peter Thiel's Mithril Capital and Khosla Ventures. The company's initial product offerings included a mobile banking app, a debit card, and a savings account. Varo Money's early success was driven by its ability to attract a large user base and generate significant revenue through interchange fees.

### Expansion Plans

As the company grew, Varo Money expanded its product offerings to include a credit card and a personal loan product. The company also launched a series of marketing campaigns to attract new customers and increase brand awareness. Varo Money's expansion plans were ambitious, with the company aiming to become a full-fledged digital bank with a wide range of financial products and services.

## The Rise — Growth, funding, traction

Varo Money experienced rapid growth in the early 2010s, with the company's user base expanding from a few thousand to over 1 million customers. The company's revenue also increased significantly, driven by the growth in interchange fees and interest income from its savings accounts. Varo Money's success attracted the attention of investors, who provided the company with additional funding to support its expansion plans.

Varo Money secured multiple funding rounds from investors, including a $45 million Series C round in 2017 and a $240 million Series D round in 2020. The company's total funding raised exceeded $500 million, making it one of the best-funded fintech companies in the industry.

In 2017, Varo Money received a banking charter from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), which allowed the company to offer a wider range of financial products and services. The charter also enabled Varo Money to expand its customer base and increase its revenue.

Despite its rapid growth and success, Varo Money faced several challenges that would ultimately contribute to its demise. One of the key warning signs was the company's cash burn rate, which increased significantly in the years leading up to its shutdown. Varo Money's cash burn rate was driven by its aggressive expansion plans, which included the launch of new products and services and the hiring of additional staff.

### Cash Burn Rate

Varo Money's cash burn rate was estimated to be around $100 million per quarter in 2020, which was significantly higher than the company's revenue growth. The company's cash burn rate was fueled by its expansion plans, including the launch of new products and services, the hiring of additional staff, and the increase in marketing expenses.

Another warning sign was Varo Money's lack of a clear business model. The company's business model was focused on generating revenue through interchange fees and interest income from its savings accounts. However, the company's revenue growth was not sufficient to support its expansion plans, and it struggled to generate significant profits.

## The Collapse — The shutdown, what happened

Varo Money's collapse was a result of a combination of factors, including cash exhaustion, inability to scale its business model, and lack of clear business model.

### Cash Exhaustion

Varo Money's cash exhaustion was a result of its aggressive expansion plans, which included the launch of new products and services and the hiring of additional staff. ### Inability to Scale Business Model

Varo Money's inability to scale its business model was a result of the company's focus on generating revenue through interchange fees and interest income from its savings accounts. The company's revenue growth was not sufficient to support its expansion plans, and it struggled to generate significant profits.

Varo Money also faced regulatory issues, including a lawsuit from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) over its overdraft practices. The lawsuit was settled out of court, but it highlighted the company's regulatory risks.
